\begin{abstract}

Cancer drug resistance is the cause of over 90\% of deaths in patients that receive targeted therapy. Even though there are large multi-omic databases (GDSC2, CCLE/DepMap), there is no computational framework that can predict resistance by reasoning over a causal hierarchy that explains how conflicting molecular signals resolve in a living cell. Existing LLM-based and deep learning approaches are either biologically opaque or are susceptible to context overwhelm and sycophantic convergence when it comes to conflicting signals from genomic, transcriptomic and pathway databases. This proposal describes the construction of a Decentralized Multi-Agent Consensus Framework inspired by Molecular Tumor Board (MTB) discussion workflows, in which four specialist agents (each connected to a dedicated Model Context Protocol (MCP) server wrapping a curated dataset) reason independently and then debate over a biological Hierarchy of Truth, with each prediction packaged alongside the full debate trace and dissent log to provide an auditable clinical accountability output.

The specific aims of this study are to determine whether an axiom-governed multi-agent debate predicts cancer drug resistance more accurately than a monolithic LLM system and other ML baselines (target AUROC $\geq 0.75$), and to determine which architectural components individually contribute to predictive accuracy and reasoning quality through 14 specified ablation studies. The full system achieves an AUROC of 0.819 and Cohen's $\kappa$ of 0.733 on a 31-case literature-validated benchmark, exceeding the monolithic LLM baseline (AUROC 0.656, $\kappa=0.491$) by a substantial margin. Ablation across 14 architectural variants confirms that the axiom hierarchy, debate protocol, and multi-modal evidence integration each contribute independently to predictive and reasoning quality. The resulting framework replaces opaque confidence scores with structured biological arguments, advancing the case for biologically faithful AI-assisted precision oncology.


\end{abstract}

\section{Background and Significance}%
\label{sec:intro}

For years, the clinical management of cancer was primarily determined by physiological and anatomical origin, with focus on identifying the specific type of cancer present \cite{hein2025ai, liu2025multiomics}. With the advent of precision oncology, there has been a shift in the medical paradigm to discover unique molecular characteristics of a tumor within a patient \cite{hein2025ai}. Multiomics, which is the integration of genomics, proteomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics and other omics layers, is known to play a crucial role in such transitions by providing a unique molecular signature that dictates the phenotypic drug resistance \cite{du2024advances}. Multidisciplinary Tumor Boards (MDT) or Molecular Tumor Boards (MTB) are useful in aiding the integration of such complex and high dimensional datasets to form meaningful treatment plans. These boards are conducted by human specialists such as oncologists, geneticists, pathologists and pharmacologists, who debate and resolve complex and often conflicting clinical signals \cite{han2025multiagent, lorenzo2026evaluation}. However, as the dimensionality of such data grows exponentially, such tumor boards face severe constraints with respect to throughput and cognitive overload.

There are a lot of cases where targeted therapies fail to fully overcome drug resistance at the individual patient level. This leads to deaths related to cancer to exceed over 90\% in patients receiving targeted therapies \cite{mao2025emerging}. For instance, in BRAF-mutant melanoma, majority of the patients are known to develop resistance within approximately two years of receiving BRAF inhibitor therapy \cite{proietti2020mechanisms}. In the same way, in EGFR-driven lung cancer, the resistance to first and second-generation EGFR inhibitors emerges routinely within 9 to 13 months \cite{rotow2017understanding}. With the high diversity in underlying molecular mechanisms of resistance (target gene mutations, alternative signaling pathways and epigenetic silencing of transcriptional programs \cite{housman2014drug}), no single omic layer can successfully capture the diversity. This requires the integration of multiple biological data layers \cite{liu2025multiomics}. Yet, the critical challenge still remains in resolving disagreements among these evidence layers when conflicting signals emerge.    

Existing computational methods in Cancer Drug Response (CDR) prediction are known to fall into the following categories:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Deep Learning and Fusion Frameworks:}Frameworks like MSDRP and DRN-CDR make use of deep residual networks and similarity network fusion for capturing non-linear features from multi-omic data \cite{saranya2024drn, zhao2023msdrp}. However, such models still function as ``black-boxes'' \cite{almutiri2023predicting}. Their high predictive performance does not provide a mechanistic reasoning or causal explanations for clinical decision support \cite{chen2024innovative}. 
    
    \item \textbf{Monolithic Large Language Models (LLMs):} CellHit and other generative AI platforms use natural language reasoning for drug response predictions \cite{parca2025cellhit, carli2025learning}. However, in such designs, the use of monolithic LLMs to process dense multi-omic payloads makes the framework vulnerable to context overwhelm  and attention bias \cite{zhou2025large, meng2024application}. They fixate on the prominent DNA mutations (like BRAF V600E) while beingg blind to RNA bypass pathways. This results in inaccurate drug response predictions \cite{kim2025medical}. 
    
    \item \textbf{Multi-Agent Systems (MAS):}EvoMDT, HAO and BioAgents are achitectures that design agents specific to certain roles to reduce context dilution \cite{liu2026evomdt, codella2025demo, mehandru2025bioagents}. However, such systems with multiple agents lack grounded biological rules for conflict resolution. This forces the agents to resolve conflicts using social negotiation, weighted voting or by making use of a ``judge'' model \cite{kaesberg2025voting}. Such systems are still susceptible to sycophancy and the problem of ``Silent Agreement''. In these scenarios, herding effects and systemic conformity suppress the independent reasoning ability of each agent \cite{cui2025freemad, turpin2023language}.
\end{itemize}

The absence of an open-source computational framework that is built on the causal ordering of molecular evidence layers embedded as an executable logic is a primary gap in precision oncology AI. Mere statistical agreement among the models is not sufficent for biological accuracy. This has been the standard consensus aggregation method that is available in existing literature \cite{cui2025freemad}. While Model Context Protocol (MCP) has been used in platforms like BioinfoMCP and MCPmed to standardize semantic contracts between LLMs and structured datasets, the usage is just as a funational tool call rather than something leveraged for explaining biological causality \cite{widjaja2025bioinfomcp, flotho2026mcpmed}. 

To address these challenges, this study introduces a decentralized multi-agent consensus architecture that takes inspiration from a human tumor board \cite{zhang2025multiagent}. This design isolates distinct biological modalities into four independent agents: Genomics, Transcriptomics, Pathways and Pharmacology. Each of these agents act as independent MCP clients that query databases (cBioPortal, CCLE, KEGG and GDSC) using schema-validated API tool calls to prevent probabilistic hallucinations \cite{barretina2012cancer, iorio2016landscape}. 

The main novelty of this architecture is the transition from open-ended linguistic negotiation to an agent-to-agent consensus protocol that is also grounded by a formal biological Hierarchy of Truth. This is rooted in the central dogma of molecular biology where this five-tier hierarchy (T1 Structural Primacy $\rightarrow$ T2 Transcriptional Gate $\rightarrow$ T3 Pathway Bypass $\rightarrow$ T4 Pharmacological Prior $\rightarrow$ T5 Statistical Consensus) governs how conflict resolution happens. Lower-tier biological axioms override the higher-tier numbers. This ensures that an independent agent should mathematically and structurally prove a mechanistic override (for instance, a transcriptomic sensitivity siganl that is physically nullified by an active downstream pathway bypass).

The study establishes two primary specific aims. First, to determine if this axiom-governed multi-agent debate can predict phenotypic cancer drug resistance better than monolithic LLMs and classic machine learning baselines, with an AUROC $\geq 0.75$ and an AUPRC $\geq 0.70$. Second, to isolate the individual contributions of each of the architectural components using six different ablation studies (omitting axioms, debate rounds, typed MCP tools, domain isolation, randomized tier order and standardized majority voting). Each prediction that is output by this system is a structured record that contains the entire debate trace and a logged dissent file which preserves even the minority opinions when an agent's verdict is overridden by a higher axiom tier. The design also replaces opaque statistical confidence scores with reproducible, biologically verifyable arguments, thus introducing a scalable and auditable framework for personalized cancer therapy. 

\subsection{Review of Existing Literature}
\label{sec:lit}

\subsubsection{MultiOmics in Precision Oncology is Now Well Established, and Why}%
The field of Cancer Drug Response (CDR) prediction has evolved from static chemical-structural analysis to sophisticated deep learning frameworks that treat drug-cell line interactions as a complex multimodal fusion problem \cite{saranya2024drn, zhao2023msdrp, chen2024innovative}. Current research establishes a clear consensus that single-omics data is inherently limited due to the low correlation (0.40) between mRNA and protein levels and that integrating genomics, transcriptomics, and epigenomics significantly improves the refined accuracy of IC50 predictions \cite{du2024advances, saranya2024drn, chen2024innovative, liu2023prediction}. Landmark architectures like DRN-CDR and MSDRP have advanced the field by utilizing Deep Residual Networks (ResNets) and Similarity Network Fusion (SNF) to capture higher-order non-linear features and mitigate the curse of dimensionality \cite{saranya2024drn, zhao2023msdrp, almutiri2023predicting, liu2023prediction}. However, a pervasive ``interpretability-performance trade-off'' remains. While these black-box models achieve high AUC values, they frequently fail to provide the mechanistic reasoning required for clinical adoption, leaving the causal link between molecular features and therapeutic outcomes largely inferred \cite{zhao2023msdrp, chen2024innovative, jiang2025network, almutiri2023predicting}.   

\subsubsection{Pre-trained and Instruction Tuned LLMs for MultiOmics}
Current research in generative AI for bioinformatics has transitioned from static feature engineering to powerful, task-specific architectures like AlphaFold and Enformer, which have revolutionized structural biology and genomic interpretation \cite{hein2025ai}. However, these ``black-box'' models often struggle with multi-omic integration, frequently losing biological nuance when reconciling conflicting signals. While a microservices-driven paradigm \cite{elsayed2026generative} improves computational scalability and throughput by up to 46\%, it still faces a semantic interoperability gap. Individual AI services can exchange data but cannot autonomously debate or prioritize signals based on the specific biological context of a tumor. 
 
The transition from Automated Machine Learning (AutoML) to reasoning-based autonomy represents a pivotal shift in computational biology, as Large Language Model (LLM) agents begin to replicate the ``plan-act-reflect'' cycle of human experts \cite{martinek2025agentomics, qu2025automating}. Current research highlights that while multi-omics integration significantly outperforms single-omic models in predictive accuracy, it remains hindered by a profound Interpretability Gap \cite{zack2025ai, vavekanand2026comprehensive}. Landmark datasets like Biology-Instructions and platforms such as PromptBio have attempted to bridge this by instruction-tuning models on millions of biological sequences, yet these systems frequently encounter generalization collapse when faced with the 3D complexity of genomic data or the high-dimensional sparsity of single-cell omics \cite{martinek2025agentomics, he2025biology, ali2025genomics, dip2025llm4cell, yang2025promptbio}. Furthermore, a persistent biological bias exists due to a lack of population diversity in training data, which leads to reduced generalizability across different ethnic groups \cite{zack2025ai, dip2025llm4cell, vavekanand2026comprehensive}. 

\subsubsection{LLMs as Treatment Recommenders}
Recent scoping reviews and reproducibility studies \cite{zhou2025large, meng2024application} demonstrate that while LLMs show human-level potential in passing medical exams, their transition to real-world treatment recommendation is hindered by a clinical utility gap. Specifically, current models struggle with the iterative and messy nature of longitudinal Electronic Health Records (EHRs) \cite{zhou2025large, williams2024evaluating} and often exhibit an over-caution bias, as seen in large-scale studies where LLMs significantly lagged behind physicians in diagnostic specificity \cite{williams2024evaluating, yang2025application}. Furthermore, the hallucination and safety bottleneck remains a primary concern. Even advanced models often favor probabilistic patterns over deep disease pathophysiology \cite{meng2024application}, leading to recommendations that may be linguistically fluent but biologically inaccurate. While hybrid neuro-symbolic architectures \cite{galitsky2024personalized} and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) \cite{zhou2025large, lin2025roles} have been proposed to mitigate these risks, most existing frameworks remain focused on clinical text summarization-reading doctor's notes rather than calculating the underlying molecular drivers of a disease \cite{lin2025roles, williams2024evaluating}.  

\subsubsection{LLMs in Tumor Drug Response}
The challenge of tumor drug resistance is fundamentally a problem of information lag and biological complexity that traditional statistical methods struggle to resolve \cite{mao2025emerging}. Current research has transitioned from single-modality analysis to multimodal AI models that integrate genomics, transcriptomics, and the tumor microenvironment to achieve high predictive accuracy \cite{mao2025emerging, liu2025multiomics, wu2026leveraging, su2025applications}. Landmark frameworks such as CellHit and GeneRxGPT have demonstrated that grounding Large Language Models (LLMs) in mechanistic pathway-aware features or real-time literature retrieval significantly outperforms static, black-box models \cite{carli2025learning, lai2025inferring}. However, despite these advances, a significant translational gap remains. Models trained on in vitro cell lines often fail in real-world patients due to intra-tumor heterogeneity and the Black Box problem, where clinicians cannot decipher the underlying biological rationale behind a resistance prediction \cite{mao2025emerging, carli2025learning, su2025applications}. 

\subsubsection{LLM in MDT - single and multi-agent systems}
The digital transformation of Multidisciplinary Tumor Boards (MDTs) has transitioned from monolithic, rule-based clinical decision support toward decentralized, multi-agent architectures that mirror the collaborative nature of human medical teams \cite{liu2026evomdt, zhang2025multiagent, han2025multiagent}. Recent literature emphasizes that role-differentiated agents, specializing in diagnostics, treatment, and safety, significantly outperform single-model approaches by reducing context dilution and enhancing reasoning accountability \cite{liu2026evomdt, zhang2025multiagent, codella2025demo}. Frameworks such as EvoMDT and HAO have demonstrated high factual recall and guideline concordance by utilizing specialized agents grounded in Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) \cite{liu2026evomdt, codella2025demo}. However, despite these architectural gains, the field faces persistent bottlenecks of reasoning. Current benchmarks like MTBBench show that models perform near-randomly on complex longitudinal tasks like recurrence forecasting, and most systems struggle to move beyond surface-level text summarization to resolve the conflicting multi-omic signals inherent in precision oncology \cite{vasilev2025mtbbench, codella2025demo, lorenzo2026evaluation}.   

\subsubsection{Debate and Consensus Mechanisms in MAS}
The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into bioinformatics has shifted the field from static algorithmic tools toward dynamic, reasoning-based multi-agent systems (MAS) capable of managing complex, multi-step workflows \cite{mehandru2025bioagents, yang2025rise}. As identified in recent frameworks like BioAgents, while single-agent systems excel at conceptual genomics and tool selection, they frequently struggle with the functional execution of code and the procedural knowledge gap required for end-to-end analysis \cite{mehandru2025bioagents}. Furthermore, the iterative refinement process often touted in multi-agent collaboration has shown diminishing returns. Repeated self-correction rounds can lead to knowledge inertia or even degrade output quality, suggesting that linguistic debate is not a substitute for grounding in high-quality domain data  \cite{mehandru2025bioagents, zhou2025streamline}. 
  
This limitation is closely tied to the architectural tension between collaborative consensus and voting paradigms. While traditional Multi-Agent Debate (MAD) frameworks treat these protocols as static variables, recent empirical evaluations demonstrate a sharp performance dichotomy: consensus protocols show a performance improvement of 2.8\% on factual, knowledge-dense tasks, whereas voting protocols yield a 13.2\% optimization on abstract reasoning tasks \cite{kaesberg2025voting}. However, scaling conversational rounds blindly deteriorates system accuracy due to ``problem drift'' and ``sycophantic convergence'' \cite{kaesberg2025voting}, alongside an inherent unreliability tax where multi-agent frameworks consume 15 to 50 times more tokens than standalone models \cite{spieser2026review}. To counteract this computational overhead, contemporary architectures utilize heterogeneous agent groups to introduce epistemic diversity and combat shared bias \cite{liu2026heterogeneous}. They also deploy sparse, progressive communication topologies such as localized intra-group debate paired with hierarchical inter-group synthesis which can reduce total token expenditure by up to 46.9\% without sacrificing logical reasoning depth \cite{liu2024groupdebate}. 
    
Crucially, standard consensus-driven aggregation models rely heavily on a final ``judge'' model or majority voting, working under the flawed assumption that agreement equates to biological accuracy \cite{cui2025freemad}. This architecture is highly susceptible to the ``Silent Agreement'' problem, where systemic conformity and herding effects suppress critical, independent reasoning paths \cite{cui2025freemad}. When no clear consensus exists, standard majority voting becomes statistically random and inequitable \cite{cui2025freemad}. While advanced frameworks have introduced mathematically rigorous aggregation tools, such as using Kendall's coefficient of concordance (W) to identify discordant agents or applying multi-agent reinforcement learning (PPO) to optimize consensus paths, these methods frequently experience latency spikes and require up to 252 GB of memory \cite{han2025multiagent}.
    
This technical barrier separates the current lab-to-live execution gap, where systems performing at a 90\% accuracy tier on static examinations experience a catastrophic drop to 45 - 69\% when subjected to practice-based, unstructured clinical data \cite{spieser2026review}. Furthermore, existing autonomous workflows remain largely reactive and restricted by a top-down supervisor-worker topology, such as PromptBio's supervisor routing engine, which exhibits severe performance degradation when generating code for complex, multi-step survival analyses \cite{yang2025application}. This underscores the critical need for decentralized, peer-to-peer agent interaction spaces that implement native, reasoning-heavy backbones alongside standardized interfaces to safely bridge data operations with true biological causality. 
 
\subsubsection{MCPs in Bioinformatics and MAD}
The transition from human-mediated command-line tools to autonomous, agent-ready infrastructures represents a critical frontier in computational biology \cite{widjaja2025bioinfomcp, flotho2026mcpmed}. As identified in recent frameworks like BioinfoMCP, the bottleneck in AI-driven science has shifted from raw reasoning capability to the manual engineering required to interface Large Language Models (LLMs) with legacy bioinformatics tools \cite{widjaja2025bioinfomcp}. While the Model Context Protocol (MCP) provides a standardized semantic contract that moves beyond basic APIs to enable machine-actionable data stewardship, current implementations often struggle with Hardware-Software Co-optimization and a lack of robust Error-Aware Recovery for biological edge cases \cite{widjaja2025bioinfomcp, flotho2026mcpmed}. Furthermore, a significant research gap remains in providing an audit trail for high-stakes clinical scenarios, as most current MCP-enabled agents focus on functional execution rather than the mechanistic explainability required for regulatory trust \cite{flotho2026mcpmed}.
  
The shift from single-model prompting toward collaborative multi-agent architectures has highlighted the potential of Multi-Agent Debate (MAD) to mitigate hallucinations and enhance reasoning through iterative peer review. However, as noted in the M3MAD-Bench study \cite{li2026m3mad}, current frameworks often suffer from divergent noise in unimodal tasks and a plateau effect, where simply increasing the rounds of debate fails to reach a more accurate convergence. A critical research gap remains in the reliance on fixed roles and rigid protocols that fail to capture environment-driven feedback, leading to ``collective delusion'' where agents mutually reinforce incorrect assumptions due to a lack of grounding in external, high-fidelity data.
  

\vspace{2em}
\noindent The preceding review identifies a critical divergence in the field of precision oncology. While predictive accuracy in multi-omic integration is rising, clinical interpretability and causal grounding remain stagnant. Current architectures, ranging from monolithic LLMs to RAG-based clinical assistants, consistently struggle with probabilistic hallucination, context dilution, and a reliance on surface-level text rather than raw biological data. To bridge this gap, this thesis introduces a Decentralized Multi-Agent Consensus Framework. By utilizing the Model Context Protocol (MCP), the framework creates a live semantic bridge to structured databases (e.g., GDSC, CCLE, and cBioPortal), enabling agents to bypass uncurated text and reason directly over raw numerical biomarkers like Z-scores and mutation frequencies.

The core innovation of this work lies in the transition from open-ended linguistic debate to a Hierarchy of Truth protocol. Unlike traditional multi-agent systems that rely on weighted voting or LLM-as-a-judge mechanisms, this framework implements a deterministic consensus logic grounded in the Central Dogma of molecular biology. By requiring agents to prove mechanistic overrides, such as a DNA structural binding failure physically nullifying a transcriptomic sensitivity signal, the system replicates the deliberative rigor of a Multidisciplinary Tumor Board (MDT). This approach directly solves the ``black-box'' problem and the ``cold-start'' challenge for new drugs by grounding every clinical verdict in verifiable biological axioms rather than pattern-matching. In doing so, this research provides a scalable, auditable, and biologically faithful architecture for the next generation of personalized cancer therapy.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methodology}

This study introduces a Decentralized Multi-Agent System (DMAS) which has four specialized LLM agents. Each agent operates on a distinct omic modality and independently analyze a (cell line, drug) pair. They then submit a structured evidence pack which is used to engage in a two-round peer debate. The outcome of the debate is governed by an explicit biological axiom hierarchy. This architecture is grounded in today's clinical practice in the form of Multidisciplinary Tumor Boards (MDTs) where oncologists, geneticists, pathologists, pharmacists, etc. debate and resolve conflicting clinical signals \cite{han2025multiagent, lorenzo2026evaluation}. The central dogma of molecular biology which establishes that DNA-level structural alterations constitute the highest-level evidence of drug-target interaction also plays a crucial role here \cite{han2025multiagent, liu2025multiomics}. 

This design is implemented using Python programming language using FastMCP for a structured tool access. Google Vertex AI (specifically Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite) is used for the LLM backend. SQLite databases are built using four publicly available bioinformatics resources. Every data access by the agents is mediated using Model COntext Protocol (MCP) tool calls. This is essential for converting hallucinations from a probabilistic failure mode to a detectable protocol violation, that is, an agent cannot assert that a mutation exists if the genomics server does not return that data \cite{flotho2026mcpmed, widjaja2025bioinfomcp}.

\subsection{Dataset Sources}%
The architecture uses four publicly available bioinformatics databases which are selected to provide orthogonal evidence for the prediction of drug sensitivity (Figure~\ref{fig:external_data_sources}). It is crucial to use multiple data sources as single-omics data is heavily limited owing to its low correlation (approximately 0.40) between mRNA and protein levels. Any single data modality only captures a single dimension of the highly complex cellular state that determines drug response \cite{saranya2024drn, zhao2023msdrp}. 

\subsubsection{Genomic Data - CCLE/DepMap Somatic Mutations and Copy Number Variation}%
The Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) / DepMap project is used to retrieve somatic mutation profiles and copy number variations (CNVs). This database is maintained by the Broad Institute and the Wellcome Sanger Institute \cite{barretina2012cancer}. CCLE provides whole-exome sequencing mutation calls and CNV segment data for around 1,800 cancer cell lines which spans more than 30 cancer lineages. The COSMIC Cancer Gene Census annotates the driver mutations. For every mutation, the cell line identifier, protein change (HGVS notation), the gene symbol, mutation type (missense, frameshift, nonsense, splice site), CIVic description and the driver status are retained. 

\textbf{CIViC enrichment.} Mutation records are enriched using drug-specific clinical evidence at the query time using Clinical Interpretation of Variants in Cancer (CIVic) database \cite{griffith2017civic}. It provides a structured evidence statement for every (gene, variant, drug) triplet, linking it to either resistant or sensitive phenotypes with evidence-level ratings from A to E. Fuzzy drug-name normalization is used to perform the enrichment by reducing the query drug and the CIVic drug field to uppercase alphanumeric strings after which a bidirectional containment check is applied. So for instance, it would allow Vemurafenib to match CIVic entries for PLX4032. This type of evidence is the highest priority signal within the T1 tier where a direct clinical evidence that links the response of a drug to a mutation supersedes all other genomic inferences.

\subsubsection{Transcriptomic Data - CCLE mRNA Expression}%
The DepMap public portal \cite{barretina2012cancer} is used to retrieve CCLE RNA-sequencing profiles which is used as the expression data in this study. Expression values are recorded as log2(TPM+1) that are also z-score normalized for every gene acroll all the cell lines. This produces a cell-line-specifc deviation score. The transcriptional silencing threshold is defined by a z-score value which is below -2.0. So a gene with $z < -2.0$ is considered to be functionally absent form the given cell line's transcriptional programme. This constitutes a resistance mechanism for drugs that require target expression for efficacy. Similarly, a z-score above +1.5 is considered as a signal for overexpression for drugs that are expression-driven. High-expression gene lists are pre-filtered at $z \geq 0.5$ for scoring an active pathway and at $z \geq 1.0$ for a bypass gene detection. 

\subsubsection{Transcriptional Silencing Threshold}
The T2 agent applies a silencing gate before reasoning over expression data. The threshold is defined as:
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{silencing}(\text{gene}, \text{cell\_line})
&=
\begin{cases}
1, & z_{\text{gene}}(\text{cell\_line}) < \theta,\\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\\[1em]
\theta &= -2.0
\quad \text{(module-level constant, \texttt{SILENCING\_THRESHOLD})}.
\end{align*}

\noindent
If $\operatorname{silencing}(\text{target\_gene},\text{cell\_line})=1$,
the verdict is forced to \textbf{RESISTANT}, regardless of the drug
mechanism type.

\subsubsection{Pathway Data - KEGG Signalling Pathways} 
The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) \cite{kanehisa2000kegg} is used to retrieve singalling pathway gene membership and the topology data. For every pathway, the database stores the complete gene menbership list and any intra-pathway roles (such as kinase, effector, transcription factor, etc). Bypass routes, or alternative signalling genes within the same pathway that acn maintain downstream oncogenic signalling when a target for a drug is blocked, are also curated from KEGG diagrams. These are stored as directed \texttt{(pathway\_id, blocked\_gene)} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{bypass\_gene} triplets. This database covers 27 cancer-relevant signalling pathways that includes MAPK/ERK, PI3K/mTOR, EGFR, ABL and regulation of apoptosis. 

\subsubsection{Pharmacological Data - CTRPv2 / GDSC2 IC50 Profiles}
Two large-scale pharmacogenomic screens are used to extract the historical drug sensitivity data. The Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal v2 (CTRPv2) serves as the source for the primary validation set. The measures drug sensitivity (in the form of area under dose-response curve and IC50) across around 860 cancer cell lines and 481 compounds \cite{rees2016ctrpv2}. GDSC2 (Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer, release 2) is used as the development dataset which provides IC50 profiles across 809 cell lines and 198 compounds. This is measured at the Wellcome Sanger Institute \cite{iorio2016landscape}. The retrieved IC50 values are converted into z-scores per drug across all the tested cell lines where $z < -0.5$ indicates a SENSITIVE response and  $z > +0.5$ indicates a RESISTANT response and $|z| \leq 0.5$ is labelled as UNCERTAIN. ChEMBL is used to supplement the data for drug target information and mechanism of action \cite{mendez2019chembl}.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing and Database Construction}
All the four data sources are preprocessed and stored in the form of local SQLite datasets to enable a fast and offline tool call without having to depend on extrenal APIs at the time of inference. This is a deliberate choice that ensures reproducibility and cost control. The entire SQLite-backed architecture ensures the smooth eplication of the entire evaluation pipeline without running into query costs or depending on third-party API availability.  

\subsubsection{Genomics Database (genomics.db)}
CCLE's raw mutation calls are filtered to only retain the coding mutations that have variant allele frequency $\geq 0.1$. The driver status is added to the mutations which is derived from the COSMIC Cancer Gene Census v99. Protein changes are normalized by stripping the p. prefix and changing it to uppercase (for instance, p.V600E $\rightarrow$ V600E) so that it becomes easier for fuzzy matching with CIVic variant nomenclature. The CNV segment files are also processed to caopy number calls at the gene-level as amplification (segement mean $>2.0$), hemizygous deletion (segment mean between 0.5 and 1.0) and homozygous deletion (segment mean $<0.5$). The CIVic prediction evidence data is stored as a separate table (\texttt{predictive\_evidence}) which contains columns for gene, normalized variant, drug which is normalized to uppercase alphanumeric, clinical significance, evidence level and evidence statement. This enrichment is performed at the query time rather than during preprocessing so that it enables drug-specific filtering without pre-joining all possible triples. 

\subsubsection{Transcriptomics Database (transcriptomics.db)}
The RNA-sequencing data from CCLE ($\log_2(\mathrm{TPM}+1)$) is normalized using z-scores for every gene across all the 1,800+ cancer cell lines. The z-scores and TPM values as a result are stored in a flat expression table indexed on \texttt{(cell\_line, gene)}. A materialistic view of the genes with high expression ($z > 0.5$) is also maintained for every cell line to enable fast pathway related queries which don't require scaning the full expression data at inference time. The silencing threshold of $z = -2.0$ as a constant at the module level so that it can be adjusted without changing any of the query logic. 

\subsubsection{Pathway Database (pathways.db)}

The KEGG REST API is used to retrieve the KEGG pathway data and is stored in three tables, \texttt{pathway\_meta} (pathway identifier, name, category), \texttt{pathway\_genes} (pathway-gene membership with role and position annotations), and \texttt{bypass\_routes} (\texttt{(pathway\_id, blocked\_gene)} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{bypass\_gene} triplets). KEGG pathway diagrams are consulted to extract and store bypass routes by identifying genes that shae downstream targets with the blocked gene but also lie on independent upstream pathways. A pathway activity score (the fraction of pathway member genes with $z \geq 0.5$ in the queried cell line) is calculated and stored at the inference time. Pathways with an activity score below 0.4 are not included in the bypass analysis so that inactive pathways in the cancer context can be excluded in the evaluation of bypass routes. 

\subsubsection{Pharmacology Database (pharmacology.db)}
IC50 values from CTRPv2 and GDSC2 are converted to natural-log scale (ln IC50) and z-score normalised per drug across all tested cell lines using:

\begin{align*}
z_{\mathrm{drug}}(\mathrm{cell\_line})
&=
\frac{
\ln\!\left(\mathrm{IC}_{50}(\mathrm{cell\_line},\mathrm{drug})\right)
-\mu_{\mathrm{drug}}
}{
\sigma_{\mathrm{drug}}
},\\
\mu_{\mathrm{drug}}
&= \text{mean log-IC}_{50}\text{ across tested cell lines},\\
\sigma_{\mathrm{drug}}
&= \text{standard deviation of log-IC}_{50}\text{ across tested cell lines}.
\end{align*}

\[
\operatorname{label}(z)=
\begin{cases}
\text{\textbf{SENSITIVE}}, & z < -0.5,\\
\text{\textbf{RESISTANT}}, & z > 0.5,\\
\text{\textbf{UNCERTAIN}}, & |z| \leq 0.5.
\end{cases}
\]

Direct lookup is possible using the pre-computed sensitivity labels thatare stored alongside the raw z-scores. Drug target information and mechanism of action annotations that are sourced from CHEMBL are also stored in a \texttt{drug\_info} table. This table stores information about target genes (comma-separated HGNC symbols), primary pathway and mechanism class. In a separate database, an LLM response cache is also maintained, keyed on \texttt{(model, prompt hash)}. This eliminated any redundant LLM calls at the time of iterative evaluation runs. 

\subsubsection{Case Construction and Label Derivation}
We evaluate the system on 31 literature-validated gold-standard (cell line, drug) pairs. Each case was selected on the basis that its resistance or sensitivity phenotype has been established in at least one peer-reviewed publication. Unlike IC50-derived labels from pharmacogenomic screens, which can conflate cytostatic growth arrest with genuine resistance, these ground-truth labels reflect experimentally confirmed mechanistic outcomes. The 31 cases span diverse cancer lineages (breast, lung, colorectal, melanoma, lymphoma, and others) and drug classes (CDK4/6 inhibitors, EGFR inhibitors, BRAF inhibitors, PARP inhibitors, and others), and include both SENSITIVE ($n = 18$) and RESISTANT ($n = 13$) phenotypes.

\subsection{Model Context Protocol Servers}
The Model Context Protocol (MCP) \cite{flotho2026mcpmed, widjaja2025bioinfomcp} is a recently standardised protocol for connecting LLMs to custom defined tools and external tools through typed, schema-validated function calls. Each MCP tool server in this framework is implemented using FastMCP, a Python library that converts decorated functions into MCP-compliant tool definitions. Agents interact with MCP servers through an async Client context manager, which serialises arguments, calls the server function, and deserialises the returned JSON. The design of the architecture inherently enforces the anti-hallucination property: because every data claim an agent makes must originate from an MCP tool call, and because tool call inputs and outputs are logged by the RunLogger, it is possible to compute a per-case hallucination rate by checking whether each biomarker cited in an agent's EvidencePack was present in the tool responses for that case.

We use MCP tooling as a structural anti-hallucination layer. In conventional LLM prompting, an agent can probabilistically assert that a mutation exists, a gene is overexpressed, or an IC50 value is known without any verifiable connection to actual data. The MCP architecture converts this probabilistic failure mode into a detectable protocol violation: an agent cannot assert that BRAF V600E is present in a cell line unless the Genomics MCP server returned it. The guarantee is inherently present in the archiecture, not probabilistic - the same design principle used in database transactions.

\subsubsection{Genomics MCP Server}
Exposes three tools: \texttt{get\_mutations(cell\_line, gene, drug)}, which returns all somatic mutations for the (cell line, gene) combination, enriched at query time with CIViC predictive evidence specific to the queried drug, \texttt{get\_cnv(cell\_line, gene)}, which returns copy number variation calls, and \texttt{check\_mutation\_impact(gene, mutation)}, which returns driver status and CIViC descriptions for a specific variant. The CIViC enrichment pipeline applies bidirectional drug-name normalisation to maximise recall of relevant clinical evidence while minimising cross-drug contamination.

\subsubsection{Transcriptomics MCP Server}
Exposes three tools: \texttt{get\_expression(cell\_line, gene)}, returning TPM and z-score for a specific gene, \texttt{get\_high\_expression\_genes(cell\_line, z\_threshold)}, returning all genes above the specified z-score threshold, and \texttt{check\_silencing(cell\_line, gene)}, returning a boolean silencing flag with the threshold used. The silencing threshold ($z = -2.0$) is injected from \texttt{axiom\_rules.py} to ensure consistency between the MCP server and the T2 agent's reasoning system prompt.

\subsubsection{Pathway MCP Server}
Exposes four tools: \texttt{find\_pathways\_for\_gene(gene)}, \texttt{get\_pathway\_genes(pathway\_id)}, \texttt{check\_bypass (pathway\_id, blocked\_gene)}, and \texttt{get\_upstream\_regulators(gene)}. The bypass check is the critical T3 evidence call. It verifies whether an alternative signalling route exists that could maintain oncogenic signalling after the drug target is blocked.

\subsubsection{Pharmacology MCP Server}
Exposes three tools: \texttt{get\_ic50(cell\_line, drug)}, returning the ln IC50, z-score, and pre-computed sensitivity label, \texttt{get\_drug\_info(drug)}, returning target genes, mechanism class, and pathway, and \texttt{get\_sensitivity\_profile(drug)}, returning population-level sensitivity distributions per mutation class. The pre-computed label is the primary T4 signal. The z-score is used by the Grounded Confidence Score (GCS) computation to measure signal strength.

\subsection{Specialist LLM Agents}
Each specialist agent extended as a subclass from the BaseAgent class, which provides shared infrastructure for MCP tool calls, LLM prompt construction, response parsing, GCS computation, and RunLogger integration. All agents use the same LLM backend (Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite via Google Vertex AI), ensuring that performance differences between agents reflect differences in data modality-specific evidence rather than model capability differences. Each agent is assigned an evidence category that reflects the biological priority of its data modality.

\subsubsection{T1: Genomics Agent}
\textbf{Data Modality:} Somatic mutations, copy number variation, CIViC clinical evidence.

\noindent\textbf{Decision logic:} (1) Check for CIViC drug-specific annotation - if present, it is decisive. (2) If driver mutation present without CIViC: UNCERTAIN (functional consequence unknown without drug-specific evidence). (3) CNV amplification of target oncogene: SENSITIVE signal. (4) Homozygous deletion: RESISTANT. (5) Hemizygous deletion: UNCERTAIN (target still expressed at reduced level). (6) Wild-type in DB but gene present: UNCERTAIN with caveat that chromosomal fusions are not captured by point-mutation databases.

\noindent\textbf{Key constraint:} Wild-type status never defaults to RESISTANT. The absence of a driver mutation does not avoid drug activity through expression or IC50 mechanisms.

\subsubsection{T2: Transcriptomics Agent}
\textbf{Data Modality:} mRNA expression z-scores, high-expression gene context.

\noindent\textbf{Decision logic:} First classifies the drug's mechanism type: (A) Overexpression-driven - target expression level IS the signal (e.g. HER2 inhibitors), (B) Mutation-driven - expression level of the target gene is NOT predictive, downstream pathway activity is assessed instead, (C) Mechanism-agnostic - transcriptomics is not informative: UNCERTAIN.

\noindent\textbf{Silencing gate:} Target gene $z < -2.0$  implies functionally absent: RESISTANT signal regardless of mechanism type.

\subsubsection{T3: Pathway Agent}
\textbf{Data Modality:} KEGG signalling pathway topology, bypass route detection.

\noindent\textbf{Decision logic:} (1) Find pathways containing the drug's target gene. (2) Apply pathway activity gate: skip pathways where $<40\%$ of member genes are expressed ($z \geq 0.5$). (3) For active pathways, check for bypass routes whose genes are upregulated ($z \geq 1.0$). (4) Bypass found + expressed: RESISTANT. (5) Target in pathway, no bypass: SENSITIVE signal. (6) Target not in any pathway: UNCERTAIN.

\noindent\textbf{Self-attestation gate:} Agent must answer 4 binary questions before committing to a RESISTANT verdict (bypass gene expressed, pathway active, mechanism relevant, bypass distinct from target). Score < 3/4: verdict demoted to UNCERTAIN regardless of database result.

\subsubsection{T4: Pharmacology Agent}
\textbf{Data Modality:} Historical IC50 z-scores, drug target and mechanism data.

\noindent\textbf{Decision logic:} Anchors verdict directly to the pre-computed IC50 label (SENSITIVE/ RESISTANT/ UNCERTAIN). The IC50 label is a population-level prior derived from measured drug response. The agent reports it faithfully and notes that higher-tier molecular evidence (T1-T3) can override it.

\noindent\textbf{Role in architecture:} T4 is the most reliable signal for mechanism-agnostic drugs (chemotherapy, broad-spectrum kinase inhibitors) where genomic or pathway-level predictors are absent. It is the lowest priority in the axiom hierarchy for targeted therapies where mutation-level evidence exists.

\vspace{2em}

\noindent All four agents run simultaneously supervised by the Orchestrator and produce independent EvidencePacks before any inter-agent communication occurs. This enforces the independence requirement of the debate protocol: an agent's R1 verdict must reflect its own evidence, not influenced by what other agents found. The structured reasoning protocol embedded in each agent's system prompt enforces step-by-step evidence inclusion before stating a verdict, reducing the probability of verdict-first, justification-second reasoning which is a known failure mode of zero-shot LLM classification \cite{turpin2023language, meng2024application}.

\subsubsection{Grounded Confidence Score (GCS)}
The GCS replaces the LLM's self-reported confidence value, which has been shown to be poorly calibrated and inflated in zero-shot settings \cite{turpin2023language}, with a formula-grounded score computed from four orthogonal components:

\[
\mathrm{GCS}=0.40H+0.30E+0.20T+0.10S
\]

\begin{align*}
H &=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{
\left|
\left\{
f \in \mathcal{F} :
\operatorname{biomarker}(f)\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{MCP}}
\right\}
\right|
}{
|\mathcal{F}|
},
& |\mathcal{F}|>0,\\[1em]
0, & |\mathcal{F}|=0,
\end{cases}\\
E &=
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if the MCP response contains a non-empty field},\\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}\\
T &=
\begin{cases}
1.0, & \text{T1},\\
0.8, & \text{T2},\\
0.6, & \text{T3},\\
0.4, & \text{T4},\\
0.2, & \text{T5},
\end{cases}\\
S &\in [0,1].
\end{align*}

\noindent
Here, $\mathcal{F}$ denotes the set of key findings and
$\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{MCP}}$ denotes the raw MCP response. For the
pharmacology agent, signal strength is calculated as

\[
S_{\mathrm{pharmacology}}
=
\min\left(\frac{|z|}{3.0},\,1.0\right).
\]

\noindent\textbf{UNCERTAIN cap:}
\[
\mathrm{GCS}_{\mathrm{final}}
=
\begin{cases}
\min(\mathrm{GCS},0.50),
& \text{if the verdict is UNCERTAIN},\\
\mathrm{GCS},
& \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
The GCS formula is structured in a way that a verdict with high confidence needs both the availability of the evidence itself (E = 1.0) and grounding (H being as close to 1.0 as possible). This is also weighted by biological priority of the modality (T) and the magnitude of the observed effect (S). If an agent cites biomarkers that are not present in the respective MCP response, that agent receives $H < 1.0$ which directly penalizes hallucinatory claims in the GCS score. 

\subsection{Debate Protocol}
The debate protocol is modelled on clinical tumor board practice \cite{han2025multiagent, lorenzo2026evaluation} which is adapted using LLM agents. It comprises of two rounds of debate with the resolver as the last resort (Figure~\ref{fig:debate_protocol}). Unstructured LLM debates have 2 primary failure modes that are addressed in this new design. The first is sycophantic convergence where agents revise their verdicts toward the majority position without new evidence \cite{turpin2023language}. The second is context overwhelm where a single agent is given all the omics data simultaneously and fails to weigh conflicting signals in an appropriate manner \cite{liu2026evomdt}.

\subsubsection{T3 Self-Attestation Gate}
Before the T3 pathway agent's verdict is counted as decisive in the Round 1 consensus check, it must pass a self-attestation gate. The agent answers 4 binary questions about the quality of its own bypass evidence:

\[
\mathrm{attestation\_score}
=
\sum_{k=1}^{4} a_k,
\qquad
a_k \in \{0,1\}.
\]

\noindent\textbf{Attestation questions:}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*), leftmargin=*]
    \item The bypass gene is named and specific.
    \item The pathway is active in the queried cell line.
    \item The drug mechanism is relevant to the pathway.
    \item The bypass gene is distinct from the primary drug target.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Gate:}
\[
\mathrm{attestation\_score}<3
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{T3 verdict is demoted to \textbf{UNCERTAIN} for consensus.}
\]

\subsubsection{Round 1: Independent Analysis and Consensus Check}
In the first round, all the four agents run concurrently and independently. Every agent queries the respective MCP servers and applies modality-specific reasoning protocols. They then produce their respective EvidencePacks along with a verdict (SENSITIVE, RESISTANT, or UNCERTAIN) and a GCS confidence score. At this stage, one agent cannot see what the other agents are finding in the round. 
After all four EvidencePacks are returned, the \texttt{\_check\_consensus()} function determines whether consensus can be established without escalating to Round 2:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Decisive threshold:} An agent is decisive if its verdict is not UNCERTAIN. A single decisive agent is sufficient for R1 consensus (\texttt{len(decisive)} $\geq 1$).

    \item \textbf{T3 self-attestation gate:} The Pathway agent's verdict is considered decisive only if the self-attestation score is $\geq 3/4$. A bypass claim of low quality (score $<3$) is demoted as UNCERTAIN for that consensus round which prevents the Pathways agent from winning any case that it cannot substantiate. 

    \item \textbf{T1 veto:} If a T1 Genomics Agent has a decisive verdict that contradicts the majority verdict of all other decisive agents, the consensus fails and the case moves to Round 2. This ensures that a clear genomic signal is not being overridden by a pharmacological majority without any debate.

    \item \textbf{Majority requirement:} If multiple decisive agents are present, the majority verdict must hold strictly ($n > \operatorname{len}(\mathrm{decisive})/2$). A 1-vs-1 split between SENSITIVE and RESISTANT decisive agents fails the majority check and escalates to Round 2.
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{Round 2: Peer Critique}
If there is no consensus in R1, the DebateEngine invokes peer critique. In Round 2, each agent receives the R1 verdict and reasoning of every other agent. Rather than a tier-specific binary checklist, agents assign a single \textbf{mechanistic relevance score} $p \in [0.0, 1.0]$ to each peer's argument: 1.0 means the peer explicitly names a specific gene or biomarker value and explains the mechanistic link to drug response; 0.5 means relevant but only indirect mechanistic connection; 0.0 means generic reasoning with no specific connection to the case. This continuous scoring replaces an earlier binary checklist design because mechanistic relevance is inherently graded.

While agents enter Round 2 with their Round 1 verdict, three mechanisms allow revision:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{AxiomChallenge concession:} When a higher-tier agent challenges a lower-tier agent's verdict and the lower-tier agent cannot rebut with modality-specific evidence, the conceding agent's verdict reverts to UNCERTAIN and its confidence is multiplied by 0.65.
  \item \textbf{Explicit contradiction revision:} An agent that cites a cross-modal contradiction in its R2 reasoning may revise its verdict; this revision is penalised by a factor of 0.78 on its confidence to discourage opportunistic flipping.
  \item \textbf{Peer-pressure revision:} An agent whose mean received peer score falls below 0.35 is automatically revised to the peer majority verdict at $0.70\times$ its original confidence.
\end{enumerate}

Endorsement scores aggregate the mechanistic relevance floats:
$\text{endorsement}(p_i) = \frac{1}{|A|-1} \sum_{j \neq i} p_{ji},$
where $p_{ji}$ is the score agent $j$ assigns to agent $i$'s argument.


\subsection{Axiom Hierarchy and Conflict Resolution}
The axiom hierarchy maps the causal ordering of biological evidence types into the conflict resolution mechanism (Figure~\ref{fig:five_tier_hierarchy}). The hierarchy is built on top of the established principles of molecular biology and clinical oncology: DNA-level structural alterations are the highest-fidelity determinants of drug-target interaction state, because they directly modify the physical binding site or gene expression capacity of the drug target \cite{han2025multiagent, liu2025multiomics}. mRNA expression provides a functional-state signal but is subject to post-transcriptional regulation. Pathway topology provides mechanistic context but requires inferred connections. Pharmacological history is a population-level requirement that integrates all upstream molecular factors but lacks specificity for the individual cell line's molecular state.

\subsubsection{Resolver Algorithm}
If neither R1 nor R2 consensus is reached, the AxiomResolver selects the winning agent using a three-key lexicographic sorting mechanism:

\[
p^{*}
=
\operatorname*{arg\,max}^{\mathrm{lex}}_{p\in\mathcal{C}}
\left(
\operatorname{endorsement}(p),
\operatorname{axiom\_weight}(\operatorname{tier}(p)),
\operatorname{GCS}(p)
\right).
\]

\[
\operatorname{axiom\_weight}(t)
=
\begin{cases}
5, & t=\mathrm{T1\_STRUCTURAL},\\
4, & t=\mathrm{T2\_TRANSCRIPTIONAL},\\
3, & t=\mathrm{T3\_PATHWAY},\\
2, & t=\mathrm{T4\_PHARMACOLOGICAL},\\
1, & t=\mathrm{T5\_STATISTICAL}.
\end{cases}
\]

\[
\mathcal{C}
=
\begin{cases}
\{p : \operatorname{verdict}(p)\neq\mathrm{UNCERTAIN}\},
& \text{if decisive agents exist},\\
\{p : p\text{ is an agent}\},
& \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

The sort is lexicographic. Endorsement score is compared first, axiom tier breaks ties if any and GCS breaks further existing ties. This means a T4 agent with very high peer endorsement score can override a T1 agent with low endorsement score, which is the expected behaviour. Factual grounding as judged by peers outweighs nominal tier priority.

\noindent\textbf{WEAK T1 OVERRIDE GATE:}
A special case applies when T1 evidence is present but weakly supported:
\noindent
The confidence-weighted fallback is used only when all of the following
conditions hold:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*), leftmargin=*]
    \item There exists a decisive T1 agent $p^{*}$:
    \[
    \operatorname{tier}(p^{*})=\mathrm{T1},
    \qquad
    \operatorname{verdict}(p^{*})\neq\mathrm{UNCERTAIN}.
    \]

    \item The T1 agent has confidence below the override threshold:
    \[
    \operatorname{GCS}(p^{*})<\tau,
    \qquad
    \tau=0.70
    \quad
    (\texttt{T1\_OVERRIDE\_CONFIDENCE\_THRESHOLD}).
    \]

    \item All lower-tier decisive agents unanimously support a different verdict:
    \[
    \left|
    \left\{
    \operatorname{verdict}(p):
    \operatorname{tier}(p)\neq\mathrm{T1},
    \operatorname{decisive}(p)
    \right\}
    \right|=1,
    \]
    and that verdict differs from $\operatorname{verdict}(p^{*})$.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Confidence-weighted fallback:}
\[
v^{*}
=
\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{v}
\sum_{\{p:\operatorname{verdict}(p)=v\}}
\operatorname{GCS}(p).
\]


\noindent\textbf{CONFIDENCE DECAY AND FINAL CONFIDENCE}
\noindent
For each agent $a_i$, after the resolver selects winner $w$:

\[
c_{\mathrm{adjusted}}(a_i)
=
\begin{cases}
\operatorname{GCS}(a_i),
& \operatorname{verdict}(a_i)=\operatorname{verdict}(w),\\[0.5em]
\operatorname{GCS}(a_i)(1-\delta),
& \operatorname{verdict}(a_i)\neq\operatorname{verdict}(w),
\end{cases}
\]

\[
\delta=0.15
\qquad
(\texttt{CONFIDENCE\_DECAY\_PER\_FLIP}).
\]

\noindent\textbf{Resolver tiebreak confidence:}
\[
c_{\mathrm{final}}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}
\sum_{a_i\in\mathcal{A}}
c_{\mathrm{adjusted}}(a_i),
\]
where $\mathcal{A}$ is the set of all agents.

\noindent\textbf{Round 1 or Round 2 consensus confidence:}
\[
c_{\mathrm{final}}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}_{v}|}
\sum_{a_i\in\mathcal{A}_{v}}
\operatorname{GCS}(a_i),
\]
where
\[
\mathcal{A}_{v}
=
\left\{
a_i\in\mathcal{A}:
\operatorname{verdict}(a_i)=v_{\mathrm{consensus}}
\right\}.
\]

UNCERTAIN predictions are excluded from AUROC/AUPRC/Spearman computation, they are included in Cohen's $\kappa$ as a third label class. Coverage is defined as:

\[
\operatorname{coverage}
=
\frac{n_{\mathrm{decisive}}}{n_{\mathrm{total}}},
\]

where

\[
n_{\mathrm{decisive}}
=
\left|
\left\{
i :
v_i^{\mathrm{final}}\neq\mathrm{UNCERTAIN}
\;\land\;
y_i^{\mathrm{true}}\neq\mathrm{UNCERTAIN}
\right\}
\right|.
\]

\noindent\textbf{SENSITIVITY SCORE FOR RANKING METRICS}

AUROC and AUPRC require a continuous score. The sensitivity score maps the framework's output to a scalar where higher values indicate more SENSITIVE predictions:
   
\[
s(r)
=
\begin{cases}
c_{\mathrm{final}}(r),
& v_{\mathrm{final}}(r)=\mathrm{SENSITIVE},\\[0.5em]
1-c_{\mathrm{final}}(r),
& v_{\mathrm{final}}(r)=\mathrm{RESISTANT}.
\end{cases}
\]

\noindent\textbf{Binary ground-truth labels:}
\[
y(r)
=
\begin{cases}
1, & y_{\mathrm{true}}(r)=\mathrm{SENSITIVE},\\
0, & y_{\mathrm{true}}(r)=\mathrm{RESISTANT}.
\end{cases}
\]

\noindent\textbf{AUROC interpretation:}
\[
\mathrm{AUROC}
=
\Pr\!\left(
s(r_{\mathrm{SENSITIVE}})
>
s(r_{\mathrm{RESISTANT}})
\right).
\]


\subsubsection{Faithfulness Evaluation}
\label{subsec:faithfulness}
Faithfulness is evaluated on all 31 gold-standard cases using an LLM-as-a-judge protocol. For each agent that produces a decisive verdict in Round 1, a judge LLM, also powered by Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite, receives the agent's structured \texttt{key\_findings} and free-text reasoning. The judge decomposes the reasoning into atomic factual claims and classifies each claim as \textit{supported} (directly traceable to a retrieved \texttt{key\_finding}), \textit{inferred} (biomedical domain knowledge derived from supported evidence), or \textit{unsupported} (a specific assertion that is not present in the retrieved evidence). Faithfulness is calculated as

\[
\operatorname{Faithfulness}
=
\frac{N_{\mathrm{supported}}}
{N_{\mathrm{supported}}+N_{\mathrm{unsupported}}}.
\]

Inferred claims are excluded from the denominator.

\subsection{Baseline Comparators}
The framework is compared against two baseline systems that represent the current state of the field:


\subsubsection{Monolithic LLM Baseline}
A single LLM agent was built using Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite that receives all four modality datasets simultaneously. This input that it receives is converted from structured MCP tool outputs into a natural-language prose. There is no multi-agent decomposition or peer debate or AxiomResolver used. The agent outputs a single verdict (as SENSITIVE, RESISTANT or UNCERTAIN) and the GCS confidence score is computed from the combined context. This is a useful baseline for directly testing whether the multi-agent architecture and two-round debate protocol add value over single-model multi-omics reasoning and probes the context-overload failure mode that is described in literature \cite{liu2026evomdt}. The same GCS formula is applied so that any confidence calibration differences reflect the reasoning quality and not scoring artefacts. 

\subsubsection{Random Forest Baseline}
A Random Forest classifier is trained on 108{,}712 (cell line, drug) pairs from the CTRPv2 pharmacogenomic screen using three feature groups. (i) binary mutation flag for the drug's primary target gene, (ii) CCLE expression z-score for the target gene, and (iii) binary driver mutation flags for 21 cancer genes. Training labels are derived from
CTRPv2 sensitivity z-scores (z~$<$~$-$0.5 = SENSITIVE, z~$>$~0.5 = RESISTANT). The trained classifier is then evaluated on the 31 gold-standard cases using binary labels derived form literature, which are independent of the CTRPv2 z-score distribution. This setup represents the strongest conventional ML approach to this problem while preserving label independence between train and test. A performance improvement over this baseline demonstrates that the DMAS agents add value beyond population-level feature lookup. A decrease would provide evidence that the structured reasoning protocol introduces unnecessary errors on cases well-handled by tabular classification.

\section{Results}
The full DMAS framework was evaluated on 31 literature-validated gold-standard (cell line, drug) pairs. Evaluation uses the system's deployed configuration. Four specialist LLM agents (T1 to T4), two rounds of peer debate with mechanistic float peer scoring, and the endorsement-primary AxiomResolver. All 31 cases received a decisive verdict. The coverage is 100\%. The framework achieved 87.1\% accuracy (27/31 correct), AUROC = 0.819, AUPRC = 0.766, Cohen's $\kappa$ = 0.733 (substantial agreement, Landis-Koch scale), sensitivity (TPR) = 1.000, and specificity (TNR) = 0.714.

\subsection{Comparison to Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

The full DMAS system is compared against two classes of baselines. (i) a traditional machine-learning baseline trained on pharmacogenomic data, and (ii) a monolithic LLM baseline that receives all evidence but uses no multi-agent structure or debate.

\paragraph{Random Forest (ML baseline).}
We trained a Random Forest classifier on 108,712 (cell line, drug) pairs from the CTRPv2 pharmacogenomic screen using three feature groups per case, which are target gene mutation (binary), target gene expression z-score (CCLE), and binary driver mutation flags for 21 cancer genes. Training labels were derived from CTRPv2 z-scores (z $<$ -0.5 = SENSITIVE, z $>$ 0.5 = RESISTANT). The model was then evaluated on the same 31 gold-standard cases using labels derived from literature (independent of CTRPv2). This setup mirrors the strongest conventional ML approach to this problem.

The RF achieves AUROC = 0.475 and $\kappa$ = $-$0.016 on the 31 gold-standard cases which is below random on AUROC and near-zero agreement on $\kappa$. The failure is systematic, not incidental. Binary mutation flags cannot distinguish resistance-conferring variants (e.g, EGFR T790M) from sensitizing ones (e.g. EGFR L858R), and the population-level rules learned from CTRPv2 z-scores do not generalize to mechanistically complex cases where the resistance phenotype is published because it is an exception to the population trend.

\paragraph{Monolithic LLM baseline.}
A single LLM (same model as the DMAS agents) receives all omics evidence as natural-language prose (genomic, transcriptomic, pathway, and pharmacological) with no structured MCP tool outputs, no specialist agent decomposition, and no debate protocol. It achieves AUROC = 0.656 and $\kappa$ = 0.491. The full DMAS system improves over this baseline by 16.3 AUROC points and 24.2 $\kappa$ points, demonstrating that the multi-agent structure and debate protocol are not just cosmetic. They produce substantively better mechanistic reasoning.

\paragraph{Published multi-omics and multi-agent models.}
Two published systems are architecturally related but address different tasks and cannot be directly compared on our benchmark (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-comparison}).
\textit{DRN-CDR}~\cite{saranya2024drn} trains a deep residual network to predict continuous IC50 values on GDSC (Pearson $r$ = 0.794, RMSE = 0.92). It performs IC50 interpolation within the GDSC training distribution and requires DNA methylation features that are not in our pipeline. It does not produce binary mechanistic verdicts. The RF proxy result (AUROC = 0.475) suggests that even a fully fitted model from the same paradigm fails on mechanistically complex out-of-distribution cases.
\textit{EvoMDT}~\cite{liu2026evomdt} is a self-evolving multi-agent system for oncology clinical treatment recommendation (MedQA-Cancer accuracy = 0.89). It shares architectural parallels with the system built in this study (specialist agent roles, evidence-grounded reasoning, conflict resolution) but targets clinical QA rather than pre-clinical mechanistic drug-response prediction. These systems are complementary rather than competing.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The full DMAS framework achieved 87.1\% accuracy ( which is 27/31 test cases correct) on the 31 gold-standard cases, with AUROC = 0.819, AUPRC = 0.766, Cohen's $\kappa$ = 0.733, coverage = 100\%, sensitivity (TPR) = 1.000, and specificity (TNR) = 0.714. A $\kappa$ of 0.733 represents substantial agreement on the Landis-Koch scale (0.61 to 0.80). Sensitivity of 1.000 (zero false negatives) is the clinically most important result. It implies that the system never falsely classifies a sensitive cell line as resistant, which in a clinical context would mean withholding an effective therapy.

\textbf{Key finding:} The DMAS achieves competitive performance without supervised training on labelled drug-resistance data. Performance is limited by database completeness and assay-type metadata gaps, not by the debate architecture or agent reasoning quality.

\subsection{The Debate Protocol: Quality as the Primary Contribution}

The most important result from the ablation study concerns the effect of removing the debate engine entirely (\texttt{no\_debate}). Contrary to earlier analysis, coverage does \emph{not} collapse. The majority-vote engine achieves 100\% coverage. This finding reframes the debate protocol's value. The two rounds of peer debate is not necessary for the system to produce a verdict on every case. It is necessary for the system to produce \textit{correct} verdicts on hard cases.

Removing debate reduces AUROC from 0.819 to 0.784 and $\kappa$ from 0.733 to 0.668, a 65-point drop in per-case agreement beyond chance. The debate is architecturally valuable not because it rescues coverage but because it suppresses overconfident single-agent verdicts in mechanistically ambiguous cases. When agents observe in Round 2 that one or more peers have committed to a verdict with stated reasoning and cited evidence, the peer critique process creates an evidence-based context that prompts agents to re-evaluate. This is not sycophantic convergence, rather it reflects a genuine evidence-backed update that multi-tier integration provides. Knowing that the pathway agent found no bypass routes (negative evidence) combined with the pharmacology agent finding a low z-score IC50 (positive evidence) gives more information than either agent alone.

\textbf{Key finding:} The debate engine's primary value is discriminative quality improvement ($\kappa$ +65 points, AUROC +3.5 points over simple majority voting), not coverage rescue.

\subsection{Comparison to Existing Methods}

The monolithic LLM baseline (\texttt{monolithic\_llm}) achieves AUROC = 0.656 and $\kappa$ = 0.491. The full DMAS system improves over this baseline by 16.3 AUROC points and 24.2 $\kappa$ points. The structured debate and MCP grounding each contribute independently. \texttt{no\_mcp} (debate retained) gives AUROC = 0.817 and $\kappa$ = 0.537, while \texttt{no\_debate} (MCP retained) gives AUROC = 0.784 and $\kappa$ = 0.668. The largest marginal contribution is from the debate protocol ($\Delta\kappa = +0.196$ over monolithic LLM) with MCP grounding providing an additional $\Delta\kappa = +0.131$.

\subsection{Agent Role Distribution and Tier Hierarchy}

T1 Genomics drove 27 of 31 verdicts (87.1\%). This reflects the case selection methodology. The 31 gold-standard cases were specifically selected because their resistance phenotypes are established in peer-reviewed publications, and the published mechanistic evidence is precisely the mutation-level, CIViC-annotated, drug-specific evidence that T1 retrieves. In a clinical setting where a novel drug lacks a published mechanistic literature, T4 pharmacological IC50 data and T3 pathway topology would become proportionally more decisive.

The T4 Pharmacology Agent exhibits a paradox. As a standalone condition (\texttt{only\_pharmacology}), T4 achieves the highest AUROC of any single-agent condition (0.863) but relatively modest $\kappa$ (0.572). This reflects T4's strength in ranking cases by IC50 z-score (AUROC measures rank quality) combined with its failure on mechanistic edge cases involving synthetic lethality, cytostatic growth arrest and bypass blindness, situations where the IC50-derived label is technically correct but mechanistically misleading.

The T3 Pathway Agent represents the most complex tradeoff. The \texttt{no\_pathway} ablation shows reduced $\kappa$ (0.604 versus 0.733) but comparable AUROC (0.824 versus 0.819). T3 adds categorical correctness ($\kappa$) while there can be some confident incorrect calls because of occasional bypass detection misfires. Whether T3 is net positive depends on the clinical use case. Ranking potential responders favours including T3, whereas minimising confidently incorrect calls favours weighting T3 lower.

\subsection{Confidence Calibration and High-Confidence Errors}

The GCS confidence formula was designed to compute conservative scores by weighting evidence tier and signal strength in addition to raw biomarker citation rate. The empirical calibration results are mixed.

The four high-confidence errors are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}. The most concerning case is \textbf{JIMT-1 + Lapatinib} (GCS = 0.921, predicted SENSITIVE, true label RESISTANT). The genomics agent correctly identifies ERBB2 amplification (a well-established Lapatinib sensitivity marker) and produces a high-confidence SENSITIVE verdict. The resistance mechanism, PIK3CA-driven bypass activation, requires cross-referencing the somatic mutation landscape with the pathway activity profile in a way the current evidence schema does not support. This is a failure of evidence integration depth, not database coverage. The relevant data exists in CIViC and KEGG but is not synthesized across the T1 and T3 modalities.

A formally calibrated confidence score would discount GCS when the winning verdict rests on a single decisive agent without corroborating cross-tier evidence. A ``singleton penalty'' rule requiring a sole decisive agent to exceed GCS $\geq 0.80$ before producing a decisive output.

\subsection{What the Ablation Tells Us About Architecture}

\subsubsection{The debate protocol: quality, not coverage}

Removing the debate protocol (\texttt{no\_debate}) achieves 100\% coverage with the majority-vote engine. The debate's value is in improving the quality of decisions. AUROC increases from 0.784 to 0.819 and $\kappa$ increases from 0.668 to 0.733 when debate is enabled. The mechanism is the AxiomChallenge and peer-pressure revision system in which agents that would commit to a wrong verdict in isolation can be corrected when a higher-tier peer challenges them with modality-specific evidence. The 19.6\% R2 revision rate confirms that this mechanism is active.

\subsubsection{The axiom hierarchy: consistent improvement, not paradox}

Replacing the endorsement-primary axiom resolver with pure confidence-weighted selection (\texttt{no\_axioms}) achieves AUROC = 0.803 and $\kappa$ = 0.668 which are both strictly lower than the full system (0.819, 0.733). The axiom hierarchy demonstrably improves both metrics. The \texttt{random\_axiom\_order} condition ($\kappa$ = 0.616, coverage = 97\%) further confirms that the specific tier ordering matters, not merely the presence of any hierarchical constraint.

\subsubsection{The no pathway tension}

The \texttt{no\_pathway} ablation study shows that the AUROC is comparable (0.824 versus 0.819) but the $\kappa$ is reduced (0.604 versus 0.733). T3 improves the categorical correctness when present but the ranking gains are sometimes limited by occasional bypass detection misfires. The appropriate correction would not be to remove T3, but to calibrate the bypass threshold in a more conservative manner. For instance, it can be enforced that confirmation is required from both T3 and at least one other agent before T3 is allowed to drive a decisive verdict. 

The architectural implication is that these ablation results together suggest that the main architectural weakness is the dominant verdict of a single agent when all other agents abstain. A ``confidence floor for singleton verdicts'' procedure requiring the GCS of a sole decisive agent to be at least 0.80 before a decisive output can be produced can be used to reduce the confidently incorrect cases without reducing the coverage.  

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

All four MCP servers have coverage gaps for novel or poorly characterised drugs. Resistance mechanisms not represented in CIViC, KEGG, CTRPv2, or CCLE cannot be captured by any agent regardless of reasoning quality. This is the primary source of the four incorrect predictions across 31 cases and is not related to the system architecture. No amount of debate or axiom refinement can compensate for absent evidence.

The primary faithfulness failure mode is cross-tier leakage, observed in 38.3\% of decisive agents wherein agents cite mechanistically correct facts from parametric knowledge that were not returned by the assigned MCP tool call. These facts are not fabricated, but they are also not grounded in the retrieved evidence. This undermines the anti-hallucination guarantee of the MCP layer. Mitigation requires a stricter source-citation schema in \texttt{EvidencePack.key\_findings}, enforcing that every atomic claim references a specific tool response field and value.

Each case requires four Round 1 agent calls, up to twelve peer-critique calls in Round 2, and multiple MCP server queries per agent, placing total latency at approximately 30 to 90 seconds per case. This is a constraint relevant to any deployment context requiring near-real-time throughput, and scales linearly with the number of cases evaluated.

The system operates as a binary classifier producing SENSITIVE, RESISTANT, or UNCERTAIN verdicts. It does not predict a continuous IC50 value, dose-response curves, drug-combination synergy, or the distinction between acquired and intrinsic resistance. These are complementary problem formulations that the current architecture does not
address.

\section{Future Work}
An immediate architectural extension is the multi-model assessment of the debate framework. Every entity of the present system is using Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite. Power of the peer debate rounds and of AxiomResolver can depend on the type of model used. It would be interesting to assess whether the same protocol in the works with other LLMs like GPT-4o or Claude Sonnet would show that the $\Delta\kappa$ helps to understand the debate mechanics beyond simply being dependent on one specific type of model or certain parameters. 

The easiest extension of the binary classification framework is predicting the continuous patterns of pharmacological response. The existing system provides results that can be classified as SENSITIVE/RESISTANT/UNCERTAIN. It would be useful to incorporate the notion of dose-response curves, IC50 distributions, or trajectories of drug effect. In order to do this, the evidence schema will need to be enhanced with concentration-series data and the GCS formula will need to be altered.

Drug combinations may serve as another viable extension of this work. The interaction of the mechanisms of action for different drugs does not follow a simple and linear rule. In one case, a cell line is found to be resistant to drug A, but sensitive to drug B. In this case, drug combination may lead to either synergy, antagonism, or synthetic lethality depending on the common pathways and compensatory signalling. For that to be taken into account, the protocol for agents would have to be changed as well, so that each agent working on the case evaluates the overlapping mechanisms and effects of drug combinations, rather than responses to single drugs. New sources of MCP-like data such as SynergxDB and DrugCombDB would be required for the task as well. Finally, the hierarchy of axioms would have to be revised to account for cases where dominant evidence class may change depending on the combination of drugs being used.

\section{Conclusion}
The research introduces a Decentralised Multi-Agent System (DMAS) designed for predicting cancer drug resistance with a high level of differentiation (AUROC = 0.819, Cohen's $\kappa$ = 0.733 based on 31 valid literature cases) without any supervised training on resistance data. The system brings together four professional LLM representatives, in each case connected to a specific MCP server, through a strict two-round debate strategy with the help of the AxiomResolver.

The key result is that the merit of the debate method is in its discriminative quality rather than in its scope. Both the whole system and the system without debate (majority verdict) gives 100\% coverage on 31 gold-standard cases. The absence of the debate methodology leads to a decrease of $\kappa$ points by 65 (0.668 vs. 0.733) and AUROC by 3.5 points (0.784 vs. 0.819). Thus, it is possible to conclude that the contribution of debate consists in countering overly confident judgments of single agents in case of mechanism ambiguity, rather than in allowing the system to make decisions in the absence of debates.

The system's performance in sensitivity (TPR = 1.000, no false negatives observed among the 31 examples tested) is clinically paramount. None of the sensitive cell lines have been wrongly assigned to resistant category. The claim-level fidelity at 194 individual claims is found to be 86.6\%, and cross-tier leakage being discovered as a new failure type is different from a previously discussed hallucination. However, the information reported by the agents is based on the review of mechanistically accurate facts provided by computed models rather than from MCP tool calls. Among the 31 predictions, the four erroneous ones involved knowledge missing in CIVIC, KEGG, CTRPv2, and CCLE. 

The main contributions of the reported study include a debate induced mechanism for quality improvement, an anti-hallucination layer based on MCP, tier-wise method of resolution, and a comprehensive study of fourteen cases isolating the effect of every single architectural piece in the solution. AxiomResolver combines the scoring system based on peer evaluation, biological class priority, and GCS confidence to produce a final output solution accompanied with a whole debate and dissent log. The work provides a solution applicable for personalized cancer therapy that is scalable, auditable and biologically faithful for clinical decision support. 

\section{Perspectives}

The research originally set out to examine the way LLM agents may be used to mimic a Multidisciplinary Tumor Board deliberation structure. The concept behind it was much simpler. Just one LLM being fed with multi-omic data.  However, the main change was the need introduced for an axiom hierarchy. Early iterations indicated that a conflict resolution mechanism has to be added as agents converge towards a confident conclusion that may not be correct from a biological point of view. Deciding to base the resolution on the central dogma of molecular biology (a need to apply a mechanical override rather than settle for mere agreement) became a momentous design decision that turned the whole system from a consensus-based approach that leads to biological invalidity into a meaningful reasoning device that respects biology.

The final design was impacted by different challenges. This made it necessary to curate a lot of literature needed for the gold-standard evaluation set. Though the SENSITIVE reports of pharmacogenomic screens are based on the IC50 data that reflect the average for the population rather than the individual mechanistic truth, 31 cases in which the resistance trait was definitely published were built with respect to the mechanisms that could be detected by existing databases. Apart from that, the design of the T3 Pathway agent demanded one additional gate for self-attestation as it was revealed previously that a drug in the assay might receive SENSITIVE verdicts for the cases where its target gene was in the pathway, yet it was not the bottleneck or mechanism of action.

The evaluation of the concept of faithfulness has resulted in identification of a more subtle problem than hallucination namely that of cross-tier leakage, where agents provide facts which are in theory correct but which were not retrieved from their respective MCP servers. The rebuttal provided in respect of the distinction between fabricated facts and factually correct but outlined facts has caused the refinement of the protocol of judges to be able to distinguish between inference from evidence and pure inference. 

Going forward, the most important extension is replacing binary SENSITIVE/RESISTANT labels with continuous dose-response curve predictions, and expanding the gold-standard case set to include cases where resistance emerged after initial sensitivity (acquired resistance), which the current binary framework cannot capture.
